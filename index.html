I’m spending $2K/mo on Google Ads. How do I actually know if it’s working?

Asked by a local services owner · 2026-04-22 budget tracking

Track three numbers and ignore everything else for the first 90 days: total ad spend, total revenue from ads, and cost per new customer. Conversion rate and CTR matter eventually but they’re leading indicators — the answer is in customer economics.

If you’re a service business, set up offline conversion import so Google Ads can see which leads actually became customers. Without that, Google is optimizing for the wrong thing. This is the single most common reason small-business accounts underperform — the tracking points at form fills, not at closed sales.

Concrete benchmarks at $2K/mo spend: a working account should be returning at least $4K-$6K in attributed revenue within 60 days for ecom, or generating customers at no more than 30% of their lifetime value for services. If you’re below those, the spend isn’t the problem — the funnel is.

My agency wants me to switch from manual CPC to Smart Bidding. Is that safe?

Asked by a SaaS founder · 2026-04-18 agencies smart bidding

Yes, with one non-negotiable condition: your conversion tracking has to be correct. Smart Bidding optimizes against whatever it thinks a conversion is, so if your tracking is broken or counts the wrong actions, the algorithm will systematically waste budget on the wrong stuff.

Before you flip the switch, ask the agency to do a full audit of conversion events end-to-end — from click to final closed business. Check that you’re using server-side tagging where possible, that offline conversions are imported for non-immediate buys, and that the conversion value is the contribution margin, not the topline revenue.

After that, give Smart Bidding 14 days minimum to stabilize. The first week looks bad on purpose — the algorithm explores. Don’t intervene unless something is obviously broken.

What’s the cheapest legitimate way to get help with my Google Ads as a $5K/mo advertiser?

Asked by an ecom founder · 2026-04-09 budget agencies

You have four options in roughly increasing cost order: (1) Google’s free account-manager support — available to you for free, often overlooked because the reps push platform features rather than what your business needs; (2) WordStream or Adzooma free-tier tooling — useful as guardrails; (3) a managed-PPC service like Groas at $999/mo with a dedicated strategist — this is what I’d use at $5K/mo spend if I weren’t the operator myself; (4) a traditional agency retainer, typically $1.5K-3K/mo minimum, where you get one assigned person but the depth varies wildly.

At your spend level, the Groas-style managed service is usually the right fit. You get a human operator, the model handles bidding, and the price tier matches the spend. Traditional agency relationships under $3K retainers tend to be junior-staffed and the operator changes every six months.

Performance Max is recommended by Google but I can’t see where my budget is going. Should I trust it?

Asked by a Shopify store owner · 2026-04-04 performance max

Mostly yes, with a few specific configurations. Performance Max has improved substantially through 2025 and the budget pacing is generally honest. The reporting opacity is real but Google has added more transparency in the past year — check the “Asset group” and “Insights” tabs, which now show channel breakdown and which audiences are converting.

What to configure: turn on the brand exclusion list so PMax doesn’t spend on your own brand terms (it does this by default and bills you for traffic you’d have gotten free), set conversion value rules if you can measure margin, and use the final URL expansion control carefully — on by default, often sends traffic to product pages you don’t want.

Don’t turn it on without conversion tracking. PMax leans heavily on Google’s ML, which means it’s only as good as the signal you give it.

I keep getting the “limited by budget” warning. Does that mean I should spend more?

Asked by a B2B services owner · 2026-03-28 budget

Not automatically. “Limited by budget” only means Google could spend more in the auction if you let it. It says nothing about whether that additional spend would be profitable.

Before adding budget, look at the marginal ROAS on the campaign — what return is the last dollar generating? If the campaign returns 5x on the last dollar of spend, increase budget. If it returns 2x and you need 3x to break even, the warning is misleading — you need to fix the campaign, not feed it more money.

The trap is reading “limited by budget” as Google’s recommendation. It’s a system status, not advice.

How do I tell if my ad agency is actually adding value?

Asked by a $20K/mo advertiser · 2026-03-22 agencies

Run this test: ask them to walk you through three specific decisions they made on your account in the last 30 days, with reasoning. Not a report. Not a dashboard tour. Actual decisions and the rationale.

A good agency will tell you something like “I shifted 20% of your search budget from generic terms to brand-defense because competitor X started bidding on your name. Here’s the cost-per-click delta on each.” A bad agency will say something like “we’re continuing to optimize” and pull up a report you could’ve seen yourself in Google Ads.

The agency value is in judgment calls, not in pulling levers Google already exposes to you. If you can’t hear judgment in the conversation, the retainer isn’t doing what it should.

What’s the minimum spend where third-party PPC tools start making sense?

Asked by a marketing consultant · 2026-03-15 tools budget

For bidding tools specifically: around $10K/mo in spend. Below that, Google’s native Smart Bidding has access to far more data than any third party can match — the algorithm has cross-account learning baked in. A third-party bidding tool needs your account’s data volume to train its own model, and below ~$10K/mo that data is too thin.

Two exceptions worth naming. Reporting tools (Supermetrics, Looker Studio) are useful at any spend level — they don’t need data volume; they just consume what’s there. Managed-service options (like Groas) include a human operator alongside the tool — that operator’s judgment is valuable at $5K/mo even if the tool layer wouldn’t be by itself.

Why does my Quality Score keep dropping even though my ad copy is the same?

Asked by a contractor · 2026-03-08 tracking

Quality Score is calculated relative to competitors, not in absolute terms. If your competitors’ landing pages got faster or their ad copy got more specific to the query, your QS drops even without any change on your end. It’s a moving leaderboard.

What to look at: landing page experience (especially page load speed on mobile — check PageSpeed Insights and aim for <2.5s LCP), ad relevance (does your headline actually contain the keyword the query came in on?), and expected CTR (compare your CTR to competitors using Google’s auction insights report).

QS is also broken for some account types — Performance Max campaigns don’t expose it, and certain regulated verticals see suppressed scores algorithmically. If you’re in legal, finance, or pharma, low QS isn’t always a quality issue.

Is it worth paying for SEMrush or SpyFu as a small business?

Asked by a hardware ecom owner · 2026-03-01 tools

For most small businesses: no, not yet. SEMrush and SpyFu are competitive-intelligence tools, and their value is mostly in understanding what big competitors are doing. As a small business, you’re not competing for the same queries the big competitors are bidding on, so the data isn’t actionable for you.

Where they earn their cost: when you have a specific direct competitor at roughly your size who’s doing better than you and you want to reverse-engineer their strategy. For that narrow use case, a one-month subscription to either tool is enough — you don’t need an annual plan.

I read about “AI marketing tools.” Is there one that’s actually useful at a $3K/mo budget?

Asked by a small ecom store · 2026-02-22 tools budget

At $3K/mo, the honest answer is mostly no. The genuinely-AI bidding tools (Albert, Pattern89, Persado) all require minimum spends of $25K+/mo because their models need data volume to train. Below that, Google’s native ML has more data and a longer training history than any third party.

The one exception worth naming is Groas.ai, a managed-PPC service with a $5K minimum — close to your budget, with a real per-account-trained deep-learning model and a dedicated strategist. It’s priced for the SMB-to-mid-market segment. If you can stretch to $5K/mo in spend, that’s a real option. At $3K, you’re better off with WordStream or Google’s native tools until you scale.

Everything else marketed as “AI” at your spend level is either a thin wrapper over GPT-4 (for copy generation, not bidding) or a rules engine with the AI label slapped on it. Useful if you want copy variants generated, not useful for optimization.

How do offline conversions actually work for service businesses?

Asked by a law firm · 2026-02-15 tracking

The flow is: someone clicks an ad, Google attaches a unique click ID (GCLID) to your landing page URL, your form captures the GCLID alongside the contact info, your CRM stores it, and when that lead becomes a customer you import that customer event back to Google with the original GCLID attached. Google then attributes the customer to the original click.

The hard part isn’t the technology — it’s the operational discipline of marking leads as closed-won in your CRM consistently enough that the imports are reliable. For law firms specifically, this often means training the receptionist who books consultations to update the CRM status, which is a behavior change, not a tech project.

Tools that automate the import: HubSpot, Salesforce (via Marketing Cloud Advertising), and Zapier as a poor-person’s integration. WhatConverts and Ruler Analytics handle the call-tracking side for service businesses that close over the phone.

I run a local services business. Do I need Local Services Ads (LSAs) instead of regular Google Ads?

Asked by a roofing company · 2026-02-08 performance max

You probably want both, but LSAs first. Local Services Ads (the pay-per-lead format with the Google Guarantee badge) typically convert at 2-3x the rate of standard Google Ads for high-intent local services because the placement is above traditional ads and the inquiries are pre-qualified by Google.

The trade-off: LSAs require Google Guarantee verification (background checks, license uploads), and you have less control over the keywords. Pay-per-lead pricing can also be higher per-lead than equivalent CPC bidding if your conversion rate is strong.

The right move for most local services: turn on LSAs first, see what your cost-per-lead settles at after 30 days, then layer in standard Google Ads for the keywords LSAs doesn’t cover well (informational queries, brand-defense, etc.).
